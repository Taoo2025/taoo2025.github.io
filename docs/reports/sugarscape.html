Systematic Experimentation in the Sugarscape Model: Exploring Inequality Dynamics and Social Evolution Mechanisms Name: Siyan Tao Date: March 10, 2026 Word Count: Approximately 1500 words (excluding figures and appendices) 1. Research Aim This study aims to investigate the emergence mechanisms of wealth inequality in the Sugarscape model proposed by Epstein and Axtell (1996) through systematic experimentation. The core objective is to compare macro-level stability between an 'immortal society' (Sugarscape 2 Constant Growback) and an 'iterative society' (Sugarscape 3 Wealth Distribution). This report focuses on analyzing how life cycle constraints (Rule R) transform society from 'static equilibrium' to 'statistical equilibrium'. It also explores how initial endowment and spatial contingency jointly shape class structure. 2. Research Methods The experiment was conducted using NetLogo 7.0.3 environment with Sugarscape 2 Constant Growback and Sugarscape 3 Wealth Distribution models (Wilensky, 1999). The experimental design included 45 parameter combinations. Each scenario was repeated 10 times to mitigate bias from stochastic processes (Railsback and Grimm, 2019). Model 2 (Constant Growback): Agents have infinite lifespan. They follow the G1 resource regeneration rule. Model 3 (Wealth Distribution): The model introduces the lifespan rule (Rule R). Lifespan is randomly set between 60-100 time steps. When an agent dies, it is replaced by a new agent with random attributes. Statistical Indicators: Gini coefficient (Gini, 1912) and Lorenz curves (Lorenz, 1905) were primarily used to measure inequality. 3. Experimental Results 3.1 Macro-level Inequality Trends

The experiment shows that the Gini coefficient in Model 3 is generally higher than in Model 2. The heatmaps below clearly demonstrate this pattern. Under the same resource regeneration rate, the replacement mechanism (Model 3) maintains a higher level of structural inequality. This occurs because new agents enter the system with extremely low initial resources. In an environment where resources are already occupied, a persistent underclass forms. Figure 1: Final Gini Coefficient Heatmap Figure 2: Mean Gini Coefficient Heatmap 3.2 Natural Selection and Trait Evolution In Model 2, agents do not die. The system eliminates high-metabolism individuals through harsh selection pressure. The data indicate that the average metabolism rate in Model 2 rapidly decreases and stabilizes around 1.0. In contrast, Model 3 exhibits high-frequency fluctuations in metabolism rate. This reflects the randomness continuously introduced by population replacement, which slows the pace of evolution. High-vision individuals demonstrate clear survival advantages. It is worth noting that Model 3 shows huge fluctuations in vision. This suggests that an agent's luck can determine survival even in highly mobile societies. An individual's starting point introduces more randomness.

4. Discussion 4.1 Emergent Inequality and the 'Mountain Effect' Even when initial conditions are set to be highly homogeneous, social inequality still emerges from the bottom up (Epstein and Axtell, 1996). This study discovers an 'equality paradox': when the initial resource gap narrows, the Gini coefficient may actually increase. This indicates that in the Sugarscape environment, spatial contingency—whether an agent is born near a 'sugar mountain'—becomes the primary factor determining wealth class. Its influence even exceeds individual talents such as vision and metabolism. 4.2 Static Equilibrium versus Statistical Equilibrium Model 2 demonstrates typical static equilibrium. After the population drops to the environmental carrying capacity, the class structure becomes completely rigid. In contrast, Model 3 achieves statistical equilibrium. Although the Gini coefficient shows periodic fluctuations, its macro-level distribution structure remains stably fluctuating. These fluctuations stem from the deaths of the wealthy and the release of their wealth. This breaks the permanent class lock-in of the 'immortal society'. It injects mobility into society. 4.3 The Nature of Wealth Distribution Although agent attributes may follow a normal distribution, final wealth emerges as an extremely skewed long-tail distribution. This confirms that in resource-constrained competitive systems, the Matthew effect is a structural feature of the system. It is not caused by individual differences among agents. 5. Conclusion Through comparative experiments, this study demonstrates that the life cycle mechanism (Model 3) can reset wealth accumulation through death and prevent extreme rigidity. However, it also maintains a higher level of Gini coefficient because it continuously introduces resource-poor newcomers. The luck brought by spatial location plays a dominant role in the formation of inequality. This provides powerful simulation modeling evidence for understanding inequality in human societies that arises from geography and generational turnover. References Epstein, J. M., and Axtell, R. (1996). Growing Artificial Societies: Social Science from the Bottom Up. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.
