Word Count (excluding list of references): 1038 Reflections on the Macro-Micro Scale Study of Automobility within the Context of Sociological Theory Task 1: Thematic Comparison Although both Who Wants to Drive Forever? (Piatkowski & Marshall, 2023) and Peri-Urban Extractivism (Delage & Rousseau, 2025) examine automobility, they approach its relationship with urban development from contrasting angles: the former treats automobility as a factor shaping spatial structure, whereas the latter views it as an outcome of spatial political economy. To explain this complexity, I use the Socio-Spatial Dialectic and Structuralism to illuminate how these sociological concepts critically interpret automobility’s role in urban systems. 1.1 Attitudes and the Socio-Spatial Dialectic In Who Wants to Drive Forever? (Piatkowski & Marshall, 2023), the key sociological concept is the Socio-Spatial Dialectic(SOJA, 1980), which explains the mutually reinforcing relationship between suburban built environments (Space) and societal meanings of independence (Society). Although residents express a strong desire for walkable communities, the existing suburban spatial structure imposes powerful constraints that compel car use as a daily necessity. The pursuit of the American Dream—personal autonomy, privacy, and self-reliance—materialised spatially through post-war suburbanization, single-family zoning, and federal highway expansion. These policies produced low-density landscapes where walking and transit became structurally unviable, embedding automobility as the default mobility practice. Once created, this space feeds back to shape societal expectations of driving as normal, necessary, and desirable. Planners

Delage and Rosseau demonstrate how an extractivist alliance pushes low-income groups to the peri-urban fringe, fragmenting land planning and imposing high mobility costs on residents. The built environment becomes a predatory mechanism that reinforces social inequality: land artificialisation and accumulation by dispossession generate one-shot value capture that externalises infrastructure and transport costs onto vulnerable households. Importantly, this form of urbanisation is not self-correcting; instead, it traps the region in “territorial non-development” by eroding long-term capacity for reinvestment(Delage & Rousseau, 2025). Structuralism therefore links automobility directly to the spatial logic of capital, showing that space functions as an instrument for reproducing inequality. Task 2: Conceptualization & Scenarios 2.1 Conceptualising Automobility : A Case Study of Research Who Wants to Drive Forever? (Piatkowski & Marshall, 2023) From the perspective of social space theory, Automobile Dependence is best conceptualized not as an individual preference, but as a structural necessity and a spatial fix(Harvey, 1978). This outcome is fundamentally produced by the Socio-Spatial Dialectic: social relations and spatial structures mutually constitute each other, forming a co-constructed concept(SOJA, 1980). As mentioned earlier the American Dream ideology produced the low-density suburban landscape that now constrains mobility choices and institutionalises driving as routine practice(Piatkowski & Marshall, 2023).

This conceptualization of Automobile Dependence as a co-constructed structural necessity contrasts sharply with the approach of Piatkowski and Marshall. Their framework primarily interprets automobility through older adults’ attitudes, psychosocial meaning, and personal experience of independence. While this perspective offers valuable behavioral insights, it overlooks the structural production of car dependence and its associated wider externalities—such as pollution, public health impacts, and the unequal distribution of mobility burdens. Ultimately, although the study adheres to a dialectical interpretation by identifying the tension between preference and spatial constraint, its reliance on correlational analysis prevents it from establishing the strong causal logic inherent in the co-construction concept. This methodological limitation leaves the precise mechanism of reciprocal influence and the role of macro-forces underexplored. 2.2 Scenario Development Using the Bifurcation Model Using the Bifurcation Point model from Complex Systems theory(Arcaute et al., 2015), two contrasting scenarios illustrate how automobility in ageing suburbs may evolve: Scenario 1: Path Lock-in (Baseline Inertia). Cultural values linking independence to private driving persist. Public funding continues to prioritise road infrastructure, reinforcing low-density suburban form. Structural inertia prevents meaningful mobility diversification, and rising mobility costs, environmental burdens, and safety risks remain insufficient to trigger systemic change. Scenario 2: Deconstruction and Tech Shock. Accumulating social and technological pressures push the system toward a bifurcation point(Classen et al., 2024). As elder driving safety concerns and caregiver burdens intensify, the symbolic meaning of the car begins to erode. Simultaneously, widespread adoption of Autonomous Vehicles (AVs), integrated with shared transit systems, offers a credible alternative to driving while maintaining independence. Task 3: Critique & Research Design 3.1 Critique The primary weakness of Who Wants to Drive Forever? lies in its methodological individualism. Although it highlights the tension between suburban spatial constraints and the desire for walkability, it largely excludes macro-structural forces—capital accumulation, zoning, highway policy—that produced auto-dependent landscapes in the first place(Cumbers & Gray, 2020; Harvey, 1978). Its focus on attitudes also overlooks environmental and public-health externalities, as well as issues of equity. In comparison, Peri-Urban Extractivism more effectively situates mobility outcomes within broader political-economic structures. 3.2 Research Question To address these limitations and introduce a causal mechanism that reflects structural constraints, I propose the following research question: Does a household's economic capital (e.g., income and wealth) moderate the relationship between low residential density and the reported mobility stress and financial burden associated with automobility?
