Agent-Based Model of Airbnb Short-Term Rental and Tenant Displacement in London CASA0011 · Centre for Advanced Spatial Analysis, UCL April 2026 Word count: 972 Ⅰ Partial ODD Protocol Description 1.1 Purpose and patterns 1) Purpose his model aims to explore the potential impact of Airbnb short-term rental pressure on long-term tenants in London under a policy that caps short-term letting at 90 days per year. The primary research questions are how this cap may encourage landlords to switch between short-term and long-term rental use, and how tourism-driven rental pressure may contribute to displacement in attractive urban areas. The model focuses on the interaction between landlords’ income-based decisions, tenants’ ability to pay, housing scarcity, and the tourism attractiveness of different locations. It is not intended to predict exact real-world eviction numbers, but to test whether a simple set of behavioural rules can generate tenant displacement, rent pressure, and policy-induced residential mobility. 2) patterns This model uses urban systems theory to simulate the diminishing returns of rent at the periphery of the city center, tourism-driven gentrification, and the spillover effect of short-term rental demand into surrounding residential areas, with the aim of determining whether London’s 90-day cap on short-term rentals can reduce tenant displacement. 1.2 Entities, state variables, and scales The model contains three entity types: landlords, tenants and patches. Landlords control individual dwellings and store tenure-mode, short-days-this-year and risk-tolerance. Tenants represent long-term residents and store monthly-income and home-patch. Patches provide the spatial environment and store GIS-based tourism-attractiveness and dynamic current-rent. The spatial scale is defined by the London GIS layer, while the temporal scale is daily: one tick represents one day, and the simulation runs for five years. Expected model patterns include a

At each tick, short-let bookings are realised first, followed by rent revision, affordability checks, natural tenant turnover, landlord reassessment and vacancy filling. Annual reset occurs every 365 ticks, when short-days-this-year and capped-this-year? are reset. This schedule allows the model to represent both daily housing-market interactions and yearly policy cycles. 1.4 Initialisation At initialisation, the model first clears all previously generated agents and output records. It then loads the London GIS shapefile and normalises ACT_RATE into a tourism-attractiveness index for different boroughs. This index is used to shape the spatial rent gradient and short-let potential. Housing units are evenly placed on selected London patches. To reduce computational cost and support later BehaviorSpace experiments, only 144 dwellings are generated. Each dwelling creates one landlord with random risk-tolerance, zero STR days and no capped status. Tenants are then assigned to dwellings with an initial occupancy rate of 0.85,

and their monthly-income is drawn from a lognormal distribution. Empty dwellings are set to vacant. Finally, landlords make an initial tenure decision, baseline rent is recorded, displacement and booking counters are reset, and the daily simulation starts at tick 0. 1.5 Input data London borough boundary data were obtained from the Greater London Authority’s London Datastore.1 The borough shapefile was joined with an ACT_RATE attribute derived from Airbnb activity data.2 1.6 Submodels The model contains several submodels. The landlord decision submodel compares expected short-let and long-let returns, adjusted by risk tolerance and a 10% STR vacancy discount. The tourism booking submodel assigns daily bookings probabilistically according to tourism attractiveness, demand intensity and spillover from capped nearby units. Under the 90-day cap, properties that reach 90 STR days are removed from short-let use and returned to the vacant or long-let pool until the annual reset. The rent submodel updates current-rent from housing scarcity. Tenants are displaced if rent becomes unaffordable or if their dwelling is converted to STR. Natural turnover is modelled separately and is not counted as displacement. Vacant units are then refilled by candidate tenants if affordable, while annual counters reset every 365 ticks. 1 https://data.london.gov.uk/dataset/statistical-gis-boundary-files-for-london-20od9 2 This analysis identifies highly active neighborhoods in London by collecting data from Airbnb (https://insideairbnb.com/get-the-data/) and aggregating travel POIs (https://data.geods.ac.uk/dataset/point-of-interest-data-for-the-united-kingdom) to identify areas with high short-term rental activity. Ⅱ Situation of Model Within the Literature This model is situated within the literature on short-term rentals, urban housing pressure and agent-based modelling. In recent years, platforms such as Airbnb have reshaped the relationship between housing markets and tourism. By allowing dwellings to generate income through short-term letting, Airbnb can create stronger financial incentives than conventional long-term renting. Previous studies have linked Airbnb growth to higher housing prices, rent pressure and concerns about displacement in major cities (Congiu, Pino and Rondi, 2022). This provides the wider motivation for modelling Airbnb as a possible source of residential instability. The model also draws on research showing that Airbnb has moved away from its early image as informal home-sharing. Professional hosts and multi-listing operators now play a larger role, which increases competition for limited housing resources (Bosma and van Doorn, 2024). This supports the model’s assumption that landlords behave as revenue-sensitive agents who compare expected short-let and long-let returns. Spatial studies further show that Airbnb listings are not evenly distributed across cities. They tend to cluster in high-demand, dense and gentrifying areas (Shabrina, Arcaute and Batty, 2019). Tourism-related factors, such as proximity to attractions and visitor services, can also influence listing density and pricing (Rabiei-Dastjerdi, McArdle and Hynes, 2022). These findings justify the use of GIS-based tourism attractiveness in the model. The contribution of this ABM is not to predict exact real-world eviction numbers, but to explore a possible mechanism through which a 90-day short-let cap may reshape landlord behaviours and tenant displacement. Rather than treating Airbnb pressure as a fixed neighbourhood attribute, the model represents individual landlords, tenants and spatial patches. Landlords decide whether a dwelling is used as a long-let, short-let or vacant unit. Tenants face affordability constraints and may be displaced or move voluntarily. Patches store tourism attractiveness and dynamic rent levels. More broadly, the model provides a basis for simulating tourism-driven gentrification by linking tourism attractiveness, landlord switching, rent pressure and tenant displacement.
